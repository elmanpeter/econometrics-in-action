# County Spending, Project Execution, and Development Outcomes in Turkana

**Author:** Peter Elman  
**Date:** November 6, 2026  
**Status:** Working draft for discussion. Source reconciliation and governor-plan comparisons remain outstanding.

## Abstract

This descriptive case study examines whether Turkana County's fiscal resources are translating into public investment, local economic activity, and improved household welfare. It combines official budget and expenditure records for FY2013/14–FY2024/25, selected project execution records, named conditional-grant schedules, and published poverty, health, education, and service indicators. Nominal budgets expanded, but development absorption averaged 53.9% and fluctuated sharply. Some service indicators improved while mass poverty remained severe. Partner receipts were volatile, and a one-off Tullow Oil land-rent arrears payment temporarily elevated reported county revenue. The analysis is descriptive and does not identify a causal effect of devolution or spending.

## Research Questions

1. How have Turkana's fiscal resources, development allocations, and execution changed since devolution?
2. How do selected welfare and service outcomes compare with the earliest available pre-devolution baselines?
3. How do partner finance and disaster-response programs complement county resources, and what execution gaps are visible?
4. What economic sectors, business-environment conditions, and natural-resource opportunities are supported by the evidence?
5. How do observed first-term fiscal priorities compare between the first and second governors, given incomplete second-administration data?

## Conceptual Framework

The proposed pathway is **public and partner finance → predictable cash and implementation capacity → completed and maintained assets and services → lower access and transaction costs and stronger productive capacity → improved household welfare, resilience, and a broader local revenue base**. Procurement, management, staffing, maintenance, community consent, and transparency mediate each transition. Drought, floods, locusts, disease, insecurity, remoteness, aid volatility, and county capacity can disrupt the pathway or affect outcomes independently. Absorption is a financial execution measure, not a measure of service quality or value for money.

## Data and Methods

County fiscal records cover FY2013/14–FY2024/25. Early-year values are partly narrative-derived from Controller of Budget (COB) reports; later years mainly use standardized tables. Outcome indicators come from KNBS, KDHS, CRA, and COB publications. Project entries are selected reported projects, not a complete project census. Nominal budgets are not deflated. Missing outcomes are not interpolated, and allocations, receipts, expenditures, and completed services are treated as distinct stages.

Pre-devolution baselines use the earliest available observation dated 2012 or earlier. Only some health measures have a 2012/13 baseline; many outcomes begin in 2009 or 2005/06. These are historical reference points, not a valid untreated counterfactual. The governor comparison is a budget-mix proxy, not a comparison of adopted CIDP/manifesto agendas. The second administration has only three observed fiscal years in the dataset, of which FY2022/23 is a transition-year budget.

## Results

### Fiscal execution

Across the 12 reported fiscal years, development absorption averaged 53.9%, with a range of 29.1% to 69.4%. Nominal approved budgets increased from KSh 8.1 billion in FY2013/14 to KSh 17.2 billion in FY2024/25. The rise in budget size did not translate into full execution.

### Governor-period budget mix

For the first governor's first five observed fiscal years (FY2013/14–FY2017/18), the development-budget share averaged 60.0% and development absorption averaged 57.9%. For the second governor, the two observed post-transition budgets (FY2023/24–FY2024/25) allocated 39.9% to development and averaged 64.0% absorption. This suggests a different observed mix and somewhat higher execution, but does not establish a change in stated policy agendas. Nominal amounts and incomplete windows limit the comparison.

### Welfare and services

Overall poverty fell from 94.3% in 2005/06 to 79.4% in 2015/16, then rose to 82.7% in 2022. The poverty gap fell from 46.0% in 2015/16 to 35.1% in 2022, and extreme poverty declined from 52.7% to 42.6%. Skilled birth attendance rose from 23% in 2014 to 53% in 2022. Household access to safe drinking water increased from 44.3% in 2009 to 48.2% in 2019; improved sanitation increased from 12.2% to 24.3%. These improvements coexist with high deprivation: in 2022, food poverty was 64.3%, and mean monthly consumption was KSh 4,203 per adult equivalent, the lowest county figure reported.

The baseline window matters. Poverty declined 14.9 percentage points from 2005/06 to 2015/16, increased 3.3 points from 2015/16 to 2022, and remained 11.6 points below the 2005/06 estimate over the full comparison. The earliest figure is a historical district-to-county mapping, and survey instruments differ; these changes should not be read as a steady trajectory or a causal devolution effect.

National benchmarks reinforce the scale of the remaining gap: Turkana's 2022 poverty headcount was 42.9 percentage points above Kenya's, and food poverty was 32.5 points higher. In the 2015/16 survey, secondary school net attendance was 29.1 points below the national rate. The benchmark table excludes the mapped 2005/06 poverty value and preserves each indicator's survey year and unit.

### Economic structure and own-source revenue

Agriculture, forestry, and fishing accounted for a reported 46.08% of county GCP in 2013 and 35.91% in 2020. Turkana's share of national GCP rose from 1.02% to 1.10%. Market access and connectivity remain constraints: the 2019 Rural Access Index was 16%, phone ownership was 16.6%, and internet use was 6.9%. COB reports show hospital/FIF revenue, cess, business permits, parking/transport fees, and fish-market fees among local own-source streams. In FY2022/23, cess was the largest reported ordinary OSR stream at KSh 78 million (43.8% of OSR). The Turkana Biashara Fund disbursed 3% of its FY2022/23 budget, and the Cooperative Enterprise Development Fund disbursed almost none.

Tullow Oil land-rent arrears drove the FY2023/24 revenue spike, which was not sustained. The COB FY2024/25 narrative and detailed table contain an unresolved arithmetic conflict: the narrative states total OSR of KSh 200.41 million while listing FIF of KSh 229.69 million and other OSR of KSh 171.14 million; Table 3.517 sums the latter two values to KSh 400.83 million. This working draft uses the detailed table for calculations and flags the discrepancy for verification before formal submission. A sensitivity check finds FY2024/25 OSR less recorded FIF at KSh 171.1 million, 10.9% below the FY2019/20–FY2022/23 reference mean of KSh 192.0 million; because AIA is not separately available in every year, this is not a pure recurring-revenue estimate.

### Partner finance and resilience

The partner-grant ledger records KSh 1.399 billion in allocations/commitments and KSh 320.7 million received in FY2024/25. The receipt gap is not evidence of default or loss without grant schedules, carryover rules, and expenditure records. Recorded programs include KfW's Drought Resilience Programme, World Bank agriculture and emergency-locust programs, climate-adaptation financing, and county investments in boreholes, irrigation, river protection, livestock, and fisheries. The available data do not establish the net effect of these interventions on disaster losses or recovery.

## Regression and Hypothesis Assessment

H1 is supported descriptively: development absorption was volatile and averaged 53.9%. H2 is tested on a separate 47-county panel, not as a Turkana-specific causal effect. In a county/year fixed-effects model for FY2020–FY2024, the coefficient on a 10-percentage-point increase in development absorption was -0.248 percentage points of real GCP growth (county-clustered SE 0.436; one-sided test for a positive coefficient p = 0.714). A 400-draw county-pairs bootstrap produced a 95% interval of [-0.917, 0.538], with 29.8% of draws positive. The predicted positive H2 relationship is not supported. H3 is consistent with the mixed outcome evidence. H4 is consistent with an implementation risk, but the available grant data do not test program impact.

## Recommendations

- Improve development execution and asset maintenance; report allocations, receipts, expenditure, functionality, and service outcomes separately.
- Build a fairer OSR base through reliable business, market, parking, land, and property registers; digital billing and e-receipts; simplified business permits; and transparent fee schedules. Protect access to essential health services and avoid duplicative charges on pastoral and agricultural trade.
- Convene a County-led development compact with faith-based groups, humanitarian agencies, UN bodies, NGOs, development partners, responsible investors, local businesses, cooperatives, and communities. Align multi-year support to health, nutrition, water, drought resilience, livelihoods, skills, and local enterprise; require transparent commitments, local hiring/procurement, safeguards, and independently verified outputs.
- Adopt a natural-resource and local-content strategy: verify resource potential and rights before licensing; require local employment, apprenticeships, and supplier development; capture value through livestock, fisheries, cold-chain, and agricultural processing; and direct one-off extractive receipts to transparent stabilization and community-development rules.
- Make environmental and social safeguards, pastoral access, water-use limits, community grievance channels, and funded closure plans conditions of investment. Do not budget speculative mineral royalties.

## Limitations

The analysis is observational and lacks an untreated Turkana counterfactual. Outcomes are sparse and drawn from different census and survey instruments. Nominal budgets are not inflation-adjusted; early fiscal values are partly narrative-derived; the project register is selective and lacks coordinates and asset-functionality data. Partner allocations and receipts do not show final spending. Direct local contributions from Catholic/Caritas, UNHCR, Gulf Energy, and other nonstate actors are not quantified in the local fiscal records. Governor-plan documents are not included. The second administration's five-year window is incomplete.
