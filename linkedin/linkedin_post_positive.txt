Devolution can transform Kenya's economy — and faster than most people think. The data show us how.

Over the past weeks I built a 47-county, 12-year dataset (FY2013/14–2024/25) from Controller of Budget, CRA, Treasury and KNBS records to ask one question: does the development absorption rate — the number we use to judge counties — actually predict development?

The headline answer is no. But buried in that result is the most hopeful finding of the whole study.

💡 What the evidence says

→ Money reaches counties. KES 4.36 trillion since 2013, and Treasury releases exceeded county spending in every single year. The constitutional promise has been kept.

→ Where counties actually spend on development, growth follows quickly. A 10 % increase in development spending per person is associated with ~0.4 pp faster real GCP growth the very next year (p < 0.01). Not a decade later. Next year.

→ The counties that received the most per capita — the arid north, long written off — grew 1.9 percentage points a year faster than the rest in 2020–24 and resisted the drought-era poverty shock best. Devolution's redistributive design works where it is allowed to.

→ Counties that executed their development budgets consistently for a decade show measurably smaller poverty increases. Discipline compounds.

⚠️ What is holding it back

→ Only 27 % of county spending goes to development — below the 30 % the PFM Act requires. KES 867 billion of approved development budgets went unspent.

→ Pending bills have grown from KES 35 bn to KES 183 bn. Unpaid contractors stall projects; only 30 % of flagship projects were complete in FY2022/23.

→ Absorption swings wildly year to year — 60 % of its variation is noise from cash-flow timing, procurement cycles and election years, not capacity.

🇰🇪 My conviction

None of these constraints is structural. They are choices: how budgets are set, when exchequer releases arrive, whether contractors are paid, whether the law's 30 % floor is honoured in expenditure and not just on paper.

The growth response to real development spending is fast — one fiscal year. The redistributive machinery already reaches the places that need it most. What stands between Kenya and a visible economic lift from devolution is fiscal discipline, adherence to the PFM Act, and professional execution of what is already budgeted.

Get those three right and the KES 867 billion that sat unspent becomes roads, water, health centres and markets — with a growth dividend the data say arrives within a year, not a generation.

Devolution is not the problem. Unfinished execution is. And execution is fixable.
